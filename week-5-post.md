## Large Language Model Interaction: The Art of War

---

## Overview

This assignment required selecting a classic text from Project Gutenberg,
downloading it, and interacting with a Large Language Model to gather
information, insights, and summaries from the text. I selected Sun Tzu's
*The Art of War*, translated by Lionel Giles (1910), sourced from Project
Gutenberg (eBook #132). I used Claude.ai as the LLM tool for this
interaction.

> Source: Sun Tzu. *The Art of War.* Translated by Lionel Giles.
> Project Gutenberg, 1910. https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/132[^1]

---

## The Book

*The Art of War* is a 2,400-year-old Chinese military treatise attributed
to Sun Tzu, a general serving the King of Wu in the 6th century BC. It
consists of thirteen chapters covering strategy, tactics, terrain,
intelligence, and the philosophy of conflict. The Giles translation
includes extensive commentary from historical Chinese scholars including
Tu Mu, Chang Yu, and Ts'ao Ts'ao, providing additional analytical depth
beyond the original text.

---

## Interactions with the LLM

### 1. Summary of Main Strategic Principles

I asked the LLM to summarize the main strategic principles of the text.
The response accurately surfaced the most recognized passages including
the foundations of deception, the importance of intelligence, and the
principle of winning without fighting. The LLM defaulted to culturally
familiar aphorisms rather than analytically engaging with the full
thirteen chapters. Deeper principles around logistics, resource
management, and terrain analysis received less emphasis despite occupying
significant portions of the text.

### 2. Key Figures Referenced in the Text

I asked the LLM to identify the key figures referenced. The response
correctly identified:

- Sun Tzu (Sun Wu) - Author and general
- King Ho-lu of Wu - The ruler Sun Tzu served
- Wu Tzu-hsu - The official who recommended Sun Tzu to the King
- Lionel Giles - The 1910 translator whose edition is the Gutenberg source

The LLM was less reliable on the historical Chinese commentators whose
annotations appear throughout the Giles edition, suggesting the model
weighted the primary text over the scholarly apparatus.

### 3. Application to Modern Operations Management

I asked how the principles apply to modern business and operations
management. The LLM produced competent but generic responses covering
leadership, adaptability, and competitive strategy. It did not
independently connect Sun Tzu's logistics and resource chapters to
operational domains without prompting. When I applied an aviation
operations lens, the connections became concrete:

| Sun Tzu Principle | Aviation Operations Application |
|-------------------|--------------------------------|
| Prolonged campaigns exhaust resources | Extended ground time and deferred decisions compound operational losses |
| Whoever is first in the field awaits the enemy fresh | Airlines with schedule buffers and reserve capacity absorb disruption better than those at maximum utilization |
| Make calculations before battle | Pre-flight risk assessment and dispatch release decisions |
| Adapt to terrain | Adjusting operations to weather, ATC constraints, and airport conditions |

### 4. Chapter I — Laying Plans: Plain Language Explanation

I asked the LLM to explain Chapter I in plain language. The response was
accurate and accessible, correctly identifying the five constant factors:
moral alignment, environmental conditions, terrain, commander quality,
and organizational discipline. However, the LLM softened the strategic
ruthlessness of the text, framing deception as general misdirection
rather than the deliberate calculated manipulation Sun Tzu describes. A
human military historian would have been more direct about the intent.

## Observations on LLM Patterns

The LLM demonstrated three consistent patterns across all interactions:

1. **Accuracy on well-known content** - Passages and principles that
appear frequently in popular culture were reproduced reliably.
2. **Surface-level synthesis** - Without specific prompting, responses
seemed to favor breadth over analytical depth
3. **Domain gap** - the LLM required domain-specific prompting to connect
strategic principles to operational contexts; a subject matter expert
would have made those connections without prompting.

Reading *The Art of War* through the lens of an LLM revealed an
additional and telling gap. The model accurately reproduced the
well-known strategic principles but made no reference to Sun Tzu's
observations about the five dangerous faults of commanders: recklessness,
cowardice, excessive concern for reputation, an uncontrolled temper, and
oversensitivity to criticism. These passages represent Sun Tzu's most
sophisticated contribution to leadership thinking, yet they are absent
from popular treatments of the text and correspondingly absent from the
LLM's unprompted responses. A human scholar of the text would have
flagged them as central; the LLM pattern of defaulting to culturally
familiar content left them invisible until specifically requested.

The most significant observation is that the LLM functions as a highly
capable retrieval and synthesis tool but does not independently generate
the kind of contextual insight that comes from deep domain experience.
The value of the interaction increases substantially when the user brings
specific domain knowledge to the prompts.
